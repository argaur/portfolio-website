01 · Problem

An Indian household usually holds money in many places. A savings account here. A mutual fund there. Gold in a locker. A policy nobody has read in years.

Each app shows one slice. No app shows the household. The person doing the planning has to hold the whole picture in their head, or on a spreadsheet that goes stale the day they stop updating it.

Three problems, not one

Your money is scattered

Bank apps, broker apps, insurance portals, a folder of paper. Nothing talks to anything else.

No household view

Every app is built for one person. A family plans as a unit. Most tools do not have a concept of a household at all.

Advice that ignores what you own

Generic advice does not know your family already has two term plans and no emergency fund. It repeats the same tips regardless of what is already in place.

Vittam is built for Indian households making a long term plan. It is not built for day traders watching charts.

Fragmented finances
are the normal case.

02 · Context

A typical Indian household is not one person with one portfolio. It is a parent with a pension account, a spouse with a separate set of mutual funds, a child with a Sukanya Samriddhi account, and maybe a grandparent’s fixed deposit that nobody has moved in a decade.

Each of those sits in a different app or a different paper file. Nobody adds them up. So questions that sound simple, like “are we covered if something happens to the main earner,” do not have a clear answer.

Vittam starts from the household, not the individual. One page. Every member. Every holding they have told you about.

Indian households
making a long term plan.

03 · Users

Vittam is built for Indian households making a long term plan. There was no user research and no interviews behind this. I did not build a persona deck. I built it from the situation itself: money spread across apps, no single view of the family, and generic advice that does not account for what the family already holds.

The people this is for are the ones who already keep a mental map of the family’s money and are tired of redrawing it every time someone asks a question.

Onboard the family.
Get one honest number back.

04 · Solution

Onboarding is three steps. Name the household. Add each member. Record what you currently hold. Then Vittam shows a dashboard with one health tier and one next step, never a wall of numbers.

1

Name the household

One field. “Let’s start with your family.” A household name, like “Gupta Family.”

2

Add each member

Name, relationship (self, spouse, child, parent, other), date of birth for age-based milestones, and an optional risk appetite.

3

Record what you hold

Member, instrument from a 30-instrument library, invested amount, current value. Everything else stays hidden until you ask for it.

The dashboard, and the five checks behind it

The dashboard shows a household health tier, an allocation donut colored by asset class, and exactly one nudge. Never zero nudges, never a list of ten. The tier comes from five checks.

#CheckWhat it looks for
1Every member holds somethingEach household member has at least one recorded holding.
2Emergency fund flaggedAt least one holding is marked as the household’s emergency fund.
3Parents are protectedEvery member in a parent relationship has active protection cover.
4Spread across classesHoldings span at least 3 of the 6 asset classes.
5Values are currentEvery holding has a current value recorded, nothing stale.
Getting Started 0 to 1 checks complete
On Track 2 to 3 checks complete
Strong 4 to 5 checks complete

Beyond the dashboard sits a public library of 30 instruments, five in each of six asset classes: Equity, Debt, Gold, Hybrid & Guaranteed, Real Estate, and Alternative. Each entry explains typical returns, risk level, minimum investment, tax treatment, liquidity, and who can invest. No account is needed to browse it, at /explore. A separate page, /why, lists the build decisions in public, each one paired with the alternative it beat.

Three decisions
that shaped the product.

05 · Decisions

D-002: No live prices

Values are typed in by hand. There is no live price feed from mfapi.in, CoinGecko, or a gold API. The dashboard will not match today’s market price. In exchange, there is no external service that can break the app.

The dashboard’s job is to show allocation and completeness, not today’s price. A stale number that is always available beats a live number that sometimes is not.

D-014: Encrypted in the browser

A household’s data is encrypted on their device before it reaches the server. The key comes from a passphrase set separately from login, because Google sign-in has no password to derive a key from.

A recovery code is shown once at signup. It is never stored on the server. Lose the passphrase and the recovery code both, and the data is gone for good, even from a database backup.

I will state this plainly: this stops me, the person who runs the server, from reading a household’s data. It is not a claim that the encryption cannot be broken by someone determined enough. The whole row is encrypted into one column, not field by field, and row sizes are padded so the length of the ciphertext does not leak the size of an amount or a name. The unlocked key lives only as a non-extractable key in the browser’s IndexedDB. The server still sees metadata: row counts, timestamps, activity patterns.

Two side effects followed from this. holdings.instrument_id stopped being a real foreign key, because the browser now matches it against the public instrument list instead of the database checking it. And a version column does double duty, catching replay attempts and resolving conflicts when the same household edits from two devices.

D-011: The obvious fix that was wrong

A review found that the learn_card_slug analytics property was polluted with route names for 5 of 6 nudge destinations. The obvious fix was to add 2 more instruments to the library, so every nudge would point at an instrument page.

I rejected it, for two reasons. It would only have fixed 1 of the 5 broken cases. And term insurance and an emergency fund are not asset classes. Adding them to the library would have bent the data model just to satisfy a tracking field.

The real fix was a new target_type property, with values learn_card or route. It described what the nudge actually pointed to, instead of forcing everything to look like an instrument.

The bug that taught the most, B-001. The single dashboard nudge, which is the whole point of the “close the loop” feature, never rendered in production. All 294 tests were green at the time.

The calculation was correct and tested. The card that displays it was correct and tested. The API route in between quietly left the nudge field out of its response, and the frontend degraded silently. No crash, no Sentry error.

I found it by clicking through the app by hand, because sign-up sits behind a Cloudflare Turnstile that blocks automated browsers from reaching that flow. The lesson: covering every unit is not the same as covering the system. Now the seam gets a test written before either side of it.

06 · Tech
LayerTechnologyDetails
FrontendVite · React 18 · TypeScriptTailwind CSS + shadcn components, Recharts for the allocation donut, React Router, an installable PWA with a service worker.
APIHono on Vercel FunctionsVerifies a Clerk JWT against Clerk’s JWKS on every request. Resolves household_id from the session, not from a request parameter.
AuthClerkHandles sign-up and login, including Google sign-in, which is why the encryption key is derived from a separate passphrase.
DataDrizzle ORM · Neon Postgres8 tables. Multi-tenancy is enforced in the app layer, not with Postgres row-level security.
ObservabilityPostHog · SentryProduct analytics and error tracking, both instrumented before launch, both still waiting on real traffic.
